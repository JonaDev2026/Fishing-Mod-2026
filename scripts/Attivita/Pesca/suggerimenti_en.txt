Every rod has a casting weight written in grams: it is the weight it casts well. With something much lighter or much heavier at the tip the cast gets shorter.
A long rod casts farther than a short one, with the same weight at the tip. If you need to reach offshore, look at the length first.
The nine rod types are: spinning, casting, bottom, feeder, match, carp, telescopic, sea, spod. Each one serves a family of fish.
With the wrong rod for that fish the odds drop a lot: with a match, bottom or telescopic rod a bit less, with the others much more.
Spinning, casting and sea rods are the casting rods: they are the only ones the lure really works with.
The rod holds a certain fish weight. It is written on the HUD next to the rod, together with the reel and line limits.
Carp, feeder, spod and bottom are the carp rods. With one of those in hand carp come at their full odds.
Pike, bass and perch want spinning or casting. They are the predators: casting rod and lure.
Trout and salmon are caught with spinning, casting or match. The other rods lower their odds.
At sea you need sea rods or casting rods. A match rod on the rocks is not the right choice.
The reel has a drag in kilos: it is the most it can hold. If the fish weighs more, that number is your limit.
The reel capacity is written by diameter: the thinner the line, the more metres fit on the spool.
The retrieve ratio says how much line you gather per turn. A high ratio reels in faster.
When you mount the line the reel takes only as much as it holds: the rest stays as a spare spool.
On the HUD the reel is the circle at the bottom right, with the drag ring around it.
A reel with little drag is fine for small fish: on big ones it gives way before the line does.
Reels come as spinning, casting and sea, like the rods they go on.
Changing reel also changes how many metres of line you can hold: check the metres on the HUD after mounting it.
The reel sings when the fish takes line and ticks when you are the one reeling in. By ear you know who is winning.
A big reel on a light rod is useless: what counts is always the weakest of the three parts.
There are four line types: mono, fluorocarbon, braid and sea lines.
Every line has three numbers: diameter in millimetres, the kilos it holds and the metres on the spool.
A thin line goes farther and more of it fits on the reel, but it holds less weight.
A thick line holds more but brakes the cast: a few metres less on every throw.
Line is bought by the spool. If the reel holds less, the rest stays as a separate spool to use another time.
Cut spools are in TACKLE under Lines, with the metres they have left.
Unmounting the line from the reel gives you back a spool with the metres left on it.
When the line snaps you lose three metres, the hook, the sinker, the float and the bait.
If you have a leader mounted and something gives way, the leader snaps: the main line stays whole.
Watch the metres on the HUD: with an almost empty spool, a running fish can take all your line.
The drag is set with the dpad, left and right, and has twelve positions.
With the drag fully closed the line does not give: you reel in fast, but tension rises just as fast.
With the drag open the fish takes line and pulls much less on it. It is the fish that tires, not your line.
The rule of thumb: close it when the fish is still, open it when it runs.
On the HUD the drag is the notched ring around the reel, with the percentage below.
With a fish much heavier than what you can hold, a closed drag means a certain break.
With a soft drag the metres come slowly even when you reel in: that is the price of safety.
The drag can be changed at any moment, even mid fight. In fact that is when it matters.
If the line snaps often, before buying a thicker one try opening the drag.
The drag also affects the metres you gain: closed you take more per turn, but with more tension.
Hooks go from #16, the smallest, to #1. Then come the sizes with a zero, from #1/0 to #18/0.
In every fish card, inside SPOTS, there is the range of hooks that suit it.
With a hook inside its range the fish comes at full odds. One size out and the odds already drop a lot.
Three sizes out of its range and that fish practically stops biting.
The hook also decides the size: with the small size of the range only commons come.
From the middle of the range up trophies start to come; with the largest size unique specimens too.
A big hook does not guarantee a big fish: it opens the ceiling, it does not take you there. The die always leans low.
In the shop the hooks are #16, #14, #12, #10, #8, #6, #4, #2 and #1, then the sizes with a zero.
Every hook belongs to a fish family: the right one for its family works better than a generic one.
A hook specialised for another family works worse than a generic one. If you lack its own, a neutral one is better.
Besides hooks, the same category holds jig heads, ready-made rigs, leaders and sinkers.
The jig head is hook and lead in one piece: it is for soft silicone baits.
On jig heads the number with the hash is the hook size, the grams are the lead.
The leader is the piece of line before the hook. The titanium one is for fish with teeth.
With a leader mounted, if something gives way the leader snaps and the main line stays whole. It costs less than a spool.
A sinker alone does not fish: at the tip you always need a hook, a jig head, a rig or a lure.
The sinker adds weight to the cast: with a very light bait it is the only way to reach far.
Ready-made rigs are complete terminal tackle: they have their own hook size, like a single hook.
Sinkers are measured in ounces in the catalogue, but the row also shows the real grams.
Hook, jig head, rig: only one at a time at the tip. Leader and sinker have their own separate slots.
The float has a load: light, medium or heavy. It says how much lead it carries.
On the HUD the float is drawn to scale with the real bottom of that patch of water.
If the float lies flat on the water it means the bait is touching the bottom: shorten the depth.
Float and lure are not used together: spinning, the spoon is both bait and hook.
Mounting a lure unmounts the float: they are two different ways of fishing.
A light-load float with a heavy sinker under it is not the right pair.
How big you see the float on the water is set in SETTINGS, first entry.
The float is your signal: when it dances the fish is tasting, when it goes under it has taken.
Floats come as classic, slider and waggler, in different sizes and shapes.
You can fish without a float, on the bottom: the float is there to see and to hold the bait up.
The bait depth under the float is set with the dpad, up and down.
It goes from thirteen centimetres to two and a half metres, in steps of thirteen centimetres.
On the HUD, under the dial, you read the depth you set and, with the line in the water, the real bottom.
If the bottom is less than the depth you set, the bait drags on the ground and the float lies flat.
Some fish feed near the bottom, others mid-water: if nothing bites, try changing the depth.
The depth can also be changed before the cast, with the rod in hand: it is more convenient.
Casting far the bottom changes: the same depth that worked near the bank may not work any more.
The water dial on the HUD also tells you which water it is: sea, lake or river, with its own bottom.
Spinning, depth does not count: the lure sinks and works while you reel in.
With the bait mid-water you avoid the junk on the bottom, but you lose the bottom feeders.
Natural baits come in five families: common, worms and insects, fresh, boilies and pellets, sea.
Every fish has its favourite baits: you find them in its card, inside SPOTS.
With one of its own baits the fish always bites. With another one it bites one time in three.
The bait is used up at every bite: when it runs out the hook is bare and nobody bites any more.
With RB, or Q on the keyboard, you change bait among the ones in the backpack without opening the menu.
Baits are bought by the pack: the shop row says how many you get.
A fish with no favourite baits in the catalogue bites any natural bait.
Boilies and pellets are carp stuff: almost no small fish uses them.
Sea baits are for the sea: on lakes they count as a wrong bait.
The bait row also shows the hook size it is usually used with.
Lures come in six types: spoons, spinners, minnows and poppers, bass jigs, soft plastics, sea.
Every lure carries its own hook size: it counts for the size exactly like a normal hook.
Predators rarely bite natural bait and almost only small ones: for them you need a lure.
Every predator card lists the lures it actually chases.
A lure that fish does not chase catches it one time in three, like a wrong bait.
A lure works fully only with a spinning, casting or sea rod. With the others almost nothing.
A lure is not used up like bait: it stays there until you lose it in a break.
The grams of the lure count in the cast: a heavy spoon goes much farther.
Soft plastics are usually used with a jig head, which gives them weight and a hook.
With a spoon you need no float and no depth setting: you just reel in.
Hold RT to charge the cast and release to let it go. The bar fills while you charge.
The casting bar is not linear: the first taps make a few metres, the last ones are worth a lot.
The weight at the tip is the lure, jig head and sinker. Natural bait weighs nothing in the cast.
If the weight at the tip sits inside the rod casting weight, the cast performs at its best.
With a weight too light for that rod the cast gets a lot shorter.
With a weight too heavy the cast also gets shorter: the rod cannot throw it.
If the bait lands on grass or asphalt the line comes back by itself: look for open water.
Casting distance does not change the wait or who bites: it only matters for hot spots.
A thin line runs better through the guides and lengthens the cast; a thick one brakes it.
Before casting you can walk along the shore with the rod in hand and choose your spot.
How long you wait depends on how alive the water is now, not on luck.
If the place has a fish at its optimum temperature, the wait is about a minute.
If the fish of the place are all at the edges of their range, the wait goes up to two and a half minutes.
If no fish of the place is in its temperature range, it can take six or seven minutes.
Reeling in slowly while you wait shortens the wait a little and moves the bait.
If you wait too long, change hour or change place: it is the water that is still, not your tackle.
The activity graph on the HUD tells you at a glance whether this is a good hour.
During the wait the float really does sit still: the game has not frozen.
With a float, pulling hard while the fish tastes scares it away.
In the last seconds before the bite the float dances and the pad vibrates: get ready.
When the fish takes, the float goes under, the pad vibrates hard and the prompt to set the hook appears at the bottom.
You have a second and a half to press A and set the hook. After that, the fish is gone.
If you did not set the hook, the bait stays and the wait restarts: you lost nothing but time.
There is no point pressing A early: setting the hook on nothing does not bring the bite forward.
Keep your hands on the buttons when the float starts dancing: it is the last warning.
The pad vibrates in two ways: gently when it tastes, hard when it has taken.
With no bait on the hook nobody bites: before casting check you have one.
If the bait ran out during the day, you see it at zero in the HUD circle.
Every bite uses up one bait, even when the fish then gets away.
Spinning, the bite comes while you reel in: you feel the hit on the rod.
During the fight hold RT to reel in. The bar above the drag is the line tension.
Tension goes from blue to green, yellow and red. In the red the line is at its limit.
If tension stays at the top for more than a moment, the line snaps. There is a margin, it is not instant.
Release the reel when the fish runs, take it back when it stops: that is the whole fight.
If you pull and it pulls, the line makes no metres: it stays put and loads up with tension.
The fish tires as you go: its runs get rarer and shorter, but they never stop completely.
A fish near the limit of your tackle tires much more slowly than a small one.
The last metres are the hardest: near the bank the fish sees the shore and digs in.
Under four metres the fish makes one last run: that is where big fish are lost.
With the left stick you move the rod left and right and drag the fish along.
Some fish have teeth: pike, barracuda, sharks and other predators.
A toothy fish over one kilo two hundred, with no leader, cuts your line after a few seconds.
Against toothy fish mount a leader, better if titanium: that is what it is for.
If the line snaps right after the hook-up and the message mentions teeth, you needed a leader.
Small toothy fish can be caught on bare line: it is the weight that makes the difference.
The fish card shows if it is a predator: the ones with lures almost always bite through.
The leader does not reduce your bite odds: mounting it just in case costs you nothing.
With a leader, when something gives way, you lose the leader and not the whole spool.
If you fish a water full of pike, mount the leader before the first cast.
A toothy fish does not cut at once: you have a few seconds to work out what took.
Every species has three sizes: common, trophy and unique specimen, with different weights.
The minimum weight that can come out is a bit more than half the common weight of that species.
The size ceiling is decided by the hook you have mounted, inside the fish range.
The ceiling never exceeds what your tackle holds: with light gear, no huge fish.
On the deep hot spot, offshore, the size shifts upwards.
A predator caught on natural bait comes out almost only small.
After a unique specimen, that species gives no more for twenty real minutes.
After a trophy, no more trophies of that species for five minutes.
The pause is per species: the pike unique does not block the carp unique.
At the golf course ponds only common fish come: it is a place to learn, not for records.
The keepnet has two limits: the biggest single fish it takes and the total weight.
If the fish is too big for your keepnet, the card tells you and you can only release it.
With the keepnet full the day is over: better to close it and sell.
While fishing, under the keepnet on the HUD you see the kilos already inside.
In TACKLE, under Keepnets, there is the list of today's fish.
The fish in the keepnet sells itself at the end of the day: you do nothing else.
A released fish still leaves you the points and the log entry: you only lose the money.
You carry one keepnet at a time: if you buy a bigger one, the other stays home.
Stringers are small, cheap keepnets: to start out they are perfectly fine.
A big keepnet is expensive, but it decides how much you can earn in a day.
The basic backpack holds one rod, one reel, two lines and ten items.
Items count by type, not by piece: ten identical hooks take one slot only.
A second spoon identical to one you already have takes no extra room.
The rod case adds slots for rods, reels and lines. Owning it is enough, even if it stays home.
The tackle box adds slots for items and lines. It counts wherever it is too.
The room adds up: backpack plus rod case plus tackle box.
If you own two boxes the biggest one counts, not the sum of both.
Before going out load the backpack: during the day home is closed.
What you left at home stays home: no shortcuts halfway through the day.
When in doubt take a spare hook and a spare line: those are the items you really lose.
Everything you buy ends up at home. Home has unlimited room.
In TACKLE A moves a piece between home and backpack, X sells it, Y throws it away.
Tackle resells at half price, and only from home: on the shore you cannot sell.
Selling and throwing away ask for confirmation: the second press really confirms.
While fishing the shop stays open but costs three times as much: it is the stall on the spot.
The green triangle in the shop marks what you already own: at home, in the backpack or mounted.
The level written in yellow is what you need to buy. In red it means you are not there yet.
Buying while fishing puts the piece straight into the backpack, if there is room.
Tackle prices are the catalogue ones divided by ten.
Planning ahead saves money: the stall on the spot is handy but expensive.
The licence is bought from the SPOTS tab, even from far away, and stays in your pocket.
The day starts only when you are on the spot and press Start fishing.
The licence covers the whole water, not the single stretch of shore.
The three-day licence costs less than three one-day licences.
The licence price depends on the stretch and on your level: you pay for what you can fish.
The day starts at five in the morning and lasts about two real hours.
One game minute passes every five real seconds: the day runs by itself.
At the end of the day the keepnet empties, the fish is sold and the mounted tackle goes back to the backpack.
Stop fishing, in the SPOTS tab, ends the day early: the licence ends there.
You can quit the game mid-day: next start you resume at the same hour.
Air and water temperature are on the HUD, at the top left.
Air is coldest at four in the morning and warmest around four in the afternoon.
Weather shifts the temperature by a few degrees: in full sun the water warms up.
Higher up it is colder: above fifty metres air drops half a degree every hundred metres.
Water is steadier than air: it changes slowly and does not follow every swing of the sky.
Every species has its temperature range, with an optimum value in the middle.
At its optimum temperature the fish comes at its best odds.
At the edges of its range the fish comes much less: temperature really counts.
More than four degrees outside its range, that fish does not come at all.
In cold water trout and pike come out, in warm water carp and catfish.
Every species has its hours: some feed at night, some at dawn and dusk, some in broad daylight.
Night runs from nine in the evening to five in the morning; dawn and dusk are the two three-hour bands around it.
A night fish comes very rarely by day: it is not bad luck, it is the wrong hour.
Dawn and dusk fish are the handiest: two good windows every day.
Some species feed at any hour, but never at their best: they are the steadiest ones.
The activity graph in the zone card shows the good hours of that place.
The same graph is on the HUD while fishing, with a dot on the current hour.
If the hour is wrong for every fish of the place, the wait gets very long.
The day starts at five: the first hour is already inside the dawn window.
Waiting a couple of game hours on the spot costs a few real minutes and changes everything.
Inside every water there are spots where a species lives: a hole, a bank, a reed bed.
Over its spot that fish becomes six times more likely than the others.
Hot spots are not marked on the map: you find them by casting and trying.
On the deep spots, offshore, size shifts upwards: the bigger fish come out.
In big lakes and at sea there are spots one and two hundred metres from the shore.
The middle of the lake is not empty: if you have the rod to reach it, try it.
If the same species keeps biting in one spot, you are on it: stay there.
A hot spot is worth more than better gear, when you are after one precise species.
Rare fish are caught almost only on their spots, at their hour, with their hook.
Moving the landing point by ten metres can completely change what bites.
Tournaments are in the TOURNAMENTS tab: one per species, in the water where that fish lives.
You buy the ticket from anywhere and it stays in your pocket until you are on the spot.
With a licence in your pocket or running you cannot enter a tournament.
With a ticket in your pocket you cannot buy a licence: one thing at a time.
The tournament has its own start hour and weather: when it starts, the sky changes.
Only fish of the tournament species put in the keepnet count: the others score nothing.
Bronze, silver and gold are kilo thresholds to reach, each with its own prize.
A trophy and a unique specimen give an extra prize on top of the medal.
If you withdraw, the day closes at once: no prize and no record.
Your best result stays as the tournament record and you see it again in its card.
Every fish landed goes in the log, even if you then release it.
The log keeps for every zone the fish caught there and how many times.
For every species the log records the weight record, with the bait and hook you had.
The three boxes common, trophy and unique are ticked only for the place where you caught them.
The exploration bar says how many species of that place you have already found.
The first catch of a species is worth eight times the normal points: changing fish pays off.
From the second to the fifth catch points are tripled, then they slowly drop.
A trophy is worth double points, a unique specimen triple.
It takes about three outings to level up, from start to finish.
At level one hundred all thirty-five waters are open: after that only the last terminal tackle unlocks.
